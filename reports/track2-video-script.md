# Track 2 — three-minute pitch script

Written to be spoken. Short sentences, no nested clauses, no variant nomenclature aloud — put `p.Leu737Ter` and `p.Asn1002Lys` on the slide, say "nonsense" and "missense". **Total 445 words — 2:46 at 160 wpm, 2:58 at a relaxed 150.** Counts measured, timings derived at 160 wpm.

**Structure, deliberately:** why this allele suggests these drugs · what evidence supports that link · what result would prove it wrong. The lead hypothesis is on the table by 0:40.

---

**[0:00–0:18] The child, and the lesion** · 50 words · 19s

Rhabdomyosarcoma. Growth restriction. Low muscle mass. Born at 32 weeks weighing a kilogram.

The genome gives biallelic *BUB1B*. One nonsense allele, destroyed before it makes protein. One missense, in the pseudokinase domain.

That call matched the answer key exactly. So we know *which* variants. The question is what to do.

**[0:18–0:39] What kind of broken** · 55 words · 21s

Here is the thing about that domain. It barely catalyses anything. What it does is hold a fold.

And in this class of mutation the fold is unstable, so the cell degrades the protein.

Not a broken protein. **Not enough of it.**

That distinction is the whole pitch, because the two have completely different treatments.

**[0:39–1:05] The evidence that it's rescuable** · 69 words · 26s

Somebody already tested it. They took these same mutant alleles, pushed their levels back to normal — and the checkpoint worked again.

Cooling the cells does it too. Drop the temperature, the protein comes back, and so does the checkpoint.

So the target isn't *activating* BubR1. Nobody knows how to activate a scaffold.

The target is **stabilising** it. And that is something medicine already does — for Fabry disease, for amyloidosis.

**[1:05–1:45] The drugs, and the fight about them** · 107 words · 40s

Two approved drugs aim at this from opposite directions. Both are contestable.

Sodium phenylbutyrate is given to newborns, so its safety record is decades old. It is sold as a chemical chaperone. But a 2022 paper says it does not fold proteins — it slows translation. Wrong direction, for a protein we already lack.

Arimoclomol raises the cell's own chaperone capacity instead. But its approved mechanism works in the lysosome — and BubR1 is not there.

And in the closest real test, it raised a mutant protein in a child — without restoring its function.

So we are not picking a winner. We are naming the experiment that picks one.

**[1:45–2:05] What else we found** · 53 words · 20s

We also looked for anything that already moves this protein up.

Two compounds do. They stabilise BubR1 in living cells.

They sit inside an assay *titled* "Destabilization of BUB1B." The direction lives in the record, not the title.

They are invisible to anyone searching by title. We found them by opening the records.

**[2:05–2:29] How you prove us wrong** · 65 words · 24s

One experiment decides all of it.

Take this exact variant. Compare it against normal BubR1 at matched protein levels.

If it isn't less stable — we are wrong, and the drug idea dies.

If it is less stable but still broken once you restore it — we are wrong, and raising it won't help.

Only if both hold does either drug make sense. **That experiment comes first.**

**[2:29–2:46] What we are not claiming** · 46 words · 17s

This variant has never been tested. Everything here is inferred from its allele class.

We are not recommending a treatment. We are naming the cheapest experiment that could refute us — a blot.

A child with a confirmed diagnosis deserves a hypothesis specific enough to be wrong.
